Mars Lander Missions

(Updated 2023-12-09)

Following is a list of lander missions that have reached Mars — successfully and otherwise — and others that are planned, as well as short notes about each. In each section, the landers are ordered by date of touchdown or attempted descent.

Links to news organization websites on this page are provided for information purposes only and do not constitute any endorsement.

Active Landers

MSL = Mars Science Laboratory, aka Curiosity:
NASA rover mission that landed Aug. 6, 2012 (UTC; late Aug. 5 in US Pacific time) at 137.44°E 4.59°S in Gale Crater. The landing site is also known as "Bradbury Landing". The nominal mission period was 668.6 sols (i.e., one Mars year), but the rover was active on Sol 4032 of its mission (Dec. 9, 2023). Since landing, the rover has traveled more than 31 km.

  • NASA/JPL mission website
  • Rover status (nasa.gov)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2012 (nasa.gov)
  • Site name (nasa.gov)

M20 = Mars 2020, aka Perseverance (and Ingenuity helicopter):
NASA rover project that landed Feb. 18, 2021, in Jezero Crater, at 77.45°E 18.44°N. The landing site is also known as "Octavia E. Butler Landing". The nominal mission period was 668.6 sols (i.e., one Mars year), but as of Dec. 9, 2023, the rover was active on Sol 997. of its mission, as was its companion mini-helicopter. Since landing, the rover has traveled more than 22 km.

  • NASA mission website
  • Rover status (nasa.gov)
  • Landing site and site name (nasa.gov)

Planned Landers

RSP = ExoMars Rover and Surface Platform:
ESA Rosalind Franklin rover project that after several delays was scheduled for launch in September 2022 combined with the Roscosmos Kazachok lander platform. But on Mar. 17, 2022, the project was suspended due to the "impossibility" of further ESA and Roscomos cooperation. ESA subsequently determined to explore alternative methods to launch and deliver the rover to Mars (e.g., cooperation with NASA). Work has been in progress toward a possible launch in 2028.

  • ESA mission overview (esa.int)
  • Landing site (esa.int)
  • Rover name (esa.int)
  • Suspension of 2022 launch (reuters.com)
  • Possibility of 2028 launch (phys.org)

Two other Mars lander projects have been in development for sample return missions. Each project would involve two spacecraft, one delivering a lander and a sample ascent vehicle and the other an orbiter and Earth-return module.

The first such sample return project is a joint NASA-ESA mission to return samples already collected and cached by Mars 2020 Perseverance. Although it had been proposed that the components launch separately in 2027 and 2028, as of late 2023, it was reported that neither launch would happen before 2030. Further, the project was undergoing re-assessment, and it was possible that the Earth-return component could be further delayed.

The second sample return project is China's (CNSA) Tianwen-3, for which both components might launch in 2028, with sample return to Earth in mid-2031.

Past Successful Landers

VL1 = Viking Lander 1:
NASA stationary probe that touched down on July 20, 1976, at 312.05°E 22.27°N in Chryse Planitia. The landing site is also known as "Thomas Mutch Memorial Station". This was the first completely successful Mars landing. The lander was active until mission Sol 2243 (Nov. 11, 1982). Its companion orbiter operated until Aug. 7, 1980.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • MGS/MOC image, 2005 (msss.com)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2006 (uahirise.org)

VL2 = Viking Lander 2:
NASA stationary probe that touched down Sep. 3, 1976, at 134.28°E 48.27°N in Utopia Planitia. The landing site is also known as "Gerald Soffen Memorial Station". The lander was active until mission Sol 1280 (Apr. 11, 1980). Its companion orbiter operated until July 7, 1978.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • MGS/MOC image, 2005 (msss.com)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2006 (uahirise.org)

MPF = Mars Pathfinder:
NASA stationary probe that landed July 4, 1997, at 326.75°E 19.47°N in Ares Vallis. The landing site is also known as "Carl Sagan Memorial Station". The last transmission received from Mars Pathfinder lander came on mission Sol 93 (Oct. 7, 1997). The lander included a small rover called Sojourner, which traveled about 100 meters during the course of the mission.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • MGS/MOC image, 1998 (msss.com)
  • MGS/MOC image, 2005 (msss.com)
  • Site name (nasa.gov)

MERA = Mars Exploration Rover A, aka Spirit:
NASA rover that landed Jan. 4, 2004, (UTC; late Jan. 3 in US time) at 175.48°E -14.57°N in Gusev Crater. The landing site is also known as "Columbia Memorial Station". Nominal mission duration was 90 sols (i.e., to Apr. 4, 2004), but the rover continued to operate for six Earth years. In May 2009 the rover became stuck in soft soil, which prevented it from best orienting its solar panels during the upcoming winter. The last communication from Spirit was received on mission Sol 2210 (Mar. 22, 2010), and NASA officially ended the mission in May 2011. The rover had traveled about 7.7 km since landing.

  • NASA mission website
  • MGS/MOC image, 2004 (msss.com)
  • Site name (nasa.gov)

MERB = Mars Exploration Rover B, aka Opportunity:
NASA rover that landed Jan. 25, 2004, at 354.47°E -1.95°N in Meridiani Planum. The landing site is also known as "Challenger Memorial Station". Nominal mission duration was 90 sols (i.e., until Apr. 25, 2004), but the rover continued to operate for 14 Earth years. On Sol 5111 of its mission (June 10, 2018), it went into a sleep mode to wait out a "historic" dust storm. After numerous attempts to re-establish communications with the rover failed following the storm's abatement, the mission was declared at an end on Feb. 13, 2019. The rover had traveled about 45 km since landing.

  • NASA/JPL mission website
  • MGS/MOC image, 2005 (msss.com)
  • Site name (nasa.gov)

PHX = Mars Phoenix:
NASA Mars Scout stationary probe that landed May 25, 2008, at 234.25°E 68.22°N in the Vastitas Borealis. Nominal mission period was to extend into late October 2008, at which time the change of seasons at this latitude was expected to result in the lander no longer receiving sufficient sunlight for its solar panels to provide power. The last communication from the lander was received on mission Sol 156 (Nov. 2, 2008). Imagery later obtained by orbiters suggests that the weight of ice accumulated during the subsequent boreal Mars winter had broken off the lander's solar panels.

  • UAz mission website (arizona.edu)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2008 (arizona.edu)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2010 (nasa.gov)

NSYT = InSight:
NASA stationary probe that landed Nov. 26, 2018, in Elysium Planitia, at 135.62°E 4.50°N. Nominal mission period was 709 sols (one Mars year + plus 40 sols, or roughly 2 Earth years), but the lander remained active until Sol 1440 of its mission (Dec. 15, 2022), when it made its last communication with mission controllers. Dust build-up on its solar panels had reduced the probe's ability to generate power by almost 95%. On Dec. 21, 2022, the mission was declared effectively over as the possibility of further communication was deemed unlikely.

  • NASA/JPL mission website
  • Landing site (nasa.gov)
  • Landing site (nasa.gov)
  • Loss of power (nasa.gov)
  • End of mission (nasa.gov)

ZR = Zhurong:
Rover component of China's 2020 Tianwen-1 orbiter-lander-rover project. It touched down late May 14, 2021 (UTC) at 109.93°E 25.07°N in the south of Utopia Planitia, and the rover was deployed from the lander platform on May 22, 2021. (The overall project was previously known during development as Huoxing-1.) The rover was placed in hibernation mode May 18, 2022 (about 352 sols after being deployment) in advance of impending boreal winter, with expectation that activities would resume in late December 2022 when temperatures and sunlight increased. In late April 2023, it was announced that the mission team had been unable to re-establish communications with the rover, likely due to dust accumulation preventing its solar panels from generating sufficient power. The rover had traveled just over 1900 meters from its landing site.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • Landing site (bbc.com)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2021 (uahirise.org)
  • Lost communication (spacenews.com)

Past Unsuccessful Landers

The following missions either failed during descent or were unable to communicate following landing.

M2 = Mars 2 Lander:
Soviet Union stationary probe that failed during descent on Nov. 27, 1971, and impacted at about 47°E -45°N in Hellas Planitia. It was probably damaged by descending during a global dust storm. Its companion orbiter operated for several months.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)

M3 = Mars 3 Lander:
Soviet Union stationary probe that landed Dec. 2, 1971, at about 202°E -45°N in Ptolemaeus Crater in Terra Sirenum. This was the first landing attempt with any degree of success. The spacecraft made soft landing and began transmitting a test image, but fell silent after about 20 s. No further communication was received. The lander may have been damaged by descent during a global dust storm, or the dust storm may have caused a corona discharge. The companion Mars 3 Orbiter operated for several months.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2013 (nasa.gov)

M6 = Mars 6:
Soviet Union stationary probe that failed during descent Mar. 12, 1974, and impacted at about 19.42°W -23.90°N near Samara Valles. A few minutes of unreadable descent data were transmitted, but transmissions ceased in "direct proximity to the surface". The associated Mars 7 lander, scheduled to land three days earlier, failed to descend to the Mars surface, likely due to computer chip failure.

  • NSSDCA Mars 6 entry (nasa.gov)
  • NSSDCA Mars 7 entry (nasa.gov)

MPL = Mars Polar Lander:
NASA stationary probe that likely crashed on descent Dec. 3, 1999, impacting at about 195.3°W -76.1°N in the Planum Australae. Failure was mostly likely caused by premature descent engine shutdown. MPL also carried two small "Deep Space 2" microprobes to be deployed during descent and which presumably impacted about 60 km away at about 196.5°W -75.0°N.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • MGS/MOC image (msss.com)
  • JPL Deep Space page (nasa.gov)

BEA = Beagle 2:
ESA/British stationary probe deployed by the Mars Express orbiter that landed Dec. 25, 2003, at 90.43°E, 11.53°N. in Isidis Planitia. Communication with Beagle 2 was not re-established after it separated from the orbiter. It was announced in January 2015 that orbital imagery had revealed the probe landed about 5 km from its target site and was apparently intact, but that it had not fully deployed, presumably due to damage caused by the force of landing impact. The Mars Express orbiter remained in operation as of December 2023.

  • NSSDCA entry (nasa.gov)
  • ESA Mars Express mission website (esa.int)
  • MRO/HiRise images, 2015 (nasa.gov)

EDM = ExoMars 2016 Entry, Descent and Landing Demonstrator Module, aka Schiaparelli:
ESA stationary probe deployed by the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO) that failed during descent on Oct. 19, 2016, and impacted at 353.79°E 2.07°S in Meridiani Planum, in the area of NASA's MER-B Opportunity rover. The probe suffered premature parachute ejection and retrorocket shutdown during descent. The impact location is reported to have been about 5.5 km west of the planned landing point. The Trace Gas Orbiter remains in operation as of December 2023.

  • ESA ExoMars 2016 mission website (esa.int)
  • MRO/HiRISE image, 2016 (nasa.gov)
  • Crash site discovery (bbc.com)
